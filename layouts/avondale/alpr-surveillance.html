What Avondale's records show so far

Avondale's documented use of license plate reader technology reaches back to 2008. City budget records say the Police Department purchased and implemented a license plate reader system that year. Later records document additional LPR activity, intersection-based testing, a current fixed-camera system and a much larger expansion funded in FY2027.

What a budget record can and cannot prove

Budget records establish what was requested, budgeted, appropriated or described. They do not by themselves prove that every funded technology item was purchased, installed, activated, deployed or actually used.

How the documented surveillance program developed

2008

An official Avondale FY2009-10 budget document says the Police Department purchased and implemented a license plate reader system in 2008. The City reported that training was completed in September 2008 for patrol officers and volunteers. The mobile system read and stored license plates as the vehicle traveled, and the City reported that approximately 50,000 license plates had already been stored in a database for detectives to review.

FY2022

Avondale's FY2022 Final Budget reported a new Police drone program and stated that seven staff members had been trained as licensed drone pilots. The following year's Police budget also described a license plate reader program launched during FY2022 that identified two stolen vehicles. Because Avondale separately documented LPR use beginning in 2008, the FY2022 reference should not be interpreted as the beginning of all license plate reader use by the City.

FY2023

The FY2023 Police budget provides retrospective confirmation of the FY2022 license plate reader launch. It also reports that two drones were added to the Police drone program. The reviewed records do not identify a separate FY2023 dollar amount for the LPR program.

FY2024

Searches of the adopted, final, Budget in Brief, recommended and tentative FY2024 budget documents did not identify explicit Police ALPR or LPR terminology. Other camera spending appears in the budget, but those systems should not automatically be classified as license plate readers.

FY2025

The Police budget explicitly identifies an objective to test deployment of cameras acting as license plate readers at specific intersections. The reviewed budget documents do not establish a dedicated dollar amount, camera count or number actually deployed for that objective.

FY2026

Budget records identify a broader collection of Police technology, including vehicle GPS, mobile dispatch computing, investigative phone software, drones and license plate reader equipment. Police asset-replacement material includes a license plate reader among the identified equipment.

FY2027

Avondale identified 15 existing license plate reader cameras and $239,000 in ongoing State RICO funding for 58 additional cameras. Budget materials describe the objective as providing city wide coverage. The City also states that officers and dispatchers can search for vehicles nationwide through more than 140 law-enforcement agencies that share their cameras with Avondale. FY2027 also contains separate requests for the Public Safety Drone Program and Police Data Sharing.

The system extends beyond Avondale's own cameras

Avondale's FY2027 budget materials state that Police officers and dispatchers can search for vehicles nationwide through more than 140 law-enforcement agencies that share their cameras with Avondale. The City says the system can provide real-time alerts involving categories such as missing persons, vehicles associated with felonies, sex offenders, stolen vehicles and stolen license plates.

This establishes that Avondale's documented LPR capability is not limited to observations made by the City's own cameras. Additional research is still needed to document the exact agencies, sharing configuration, access rules, retention settings and audit controls governing that network.

Deployment status still needs to be verified

The FY2027 budget establishes funding authority for the 58-camera expansion. It does not by itself prove that all 58 cameras have been purchased, installed, activated or deployed.

Police drones and Drone as First Responder

16
Existing Police drones

Reported by Avondale when the FY2027 request was prepared.

Avondale's Police drone program predates the FY2027 surveillance expansion. FY2022 records describe a new Police drone program, and FY2023 records report that two drones had been added during FY2022.

$120,000 for the FY2027 Public Safety Drone Program

FY2027 budget materials discuss Drone as First Responder technology, including drones housed in remote docking stations. Avondale also stated that its existing Police drone program consisted of 16 drones when the request was prepared.

Separate program

The $120,000 Public Safety Drone Program request is separate from the $239,000 license plate reader expansion. The programs should not be combined into a single spending figure without explaining that they are distinct budget items.

What remains unknown or is still being verified

The budget record answers some questions, but it does not provide a complete picture of how Avondale's surveillance systems currently operate. Additional records are still being collected and reviewed.

  • The vendor or vendors associated with each phase of Avondale's LPR program.
  • The current number of active LPR cameras.
  • How many of the 58 FY2027-funded cameras have been ordered or installed.
  • Current camera locations and coverage.
  • Data-retention and deletion settings.
  • The exact agencies and access rules behind the 140+ law-enforcement agencies Avondale says share cameras with its system.
  • Hot-list sources and alert procedures.
  • Audit, misuse-review and access-control requirements.
  • Contracts, invoices, amendments and current recurring costs.
  • Operational details of Police Data Sharing.
  • Current Drone as First Responder deployment and operating policies.

Privacy and civil-liberties research

This section is being developed separately from the budget findings. It will examine documented ALPR misuse and wrongful stops, data-sharing risks, relevant court decisions and the privacy implications of creating searchable records of vehicle movements.

Legal claims will be kept separate from policy concerns

Court decisions involving GPS tracking, historical location data and other technologies may provide important constitutional context without necessarily deciding the legality of Avondale's particular systems. Those distinctions will be identified as the legal research is added.

Resident participation

Public meetings, written comments, public-records requests and direct questions to elected officials can help residents obtain information about surveillance technology and participate in decisions about its use.

Meeting information, contact options and specific questions residents can ask will be added here as those resources are verified and organized.
